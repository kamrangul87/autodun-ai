Why UK councils still plan EV charging from charger counts, not usability

By Autodun Research Team · Updated 9 November 2026

Local authorities are not short of dashboards. They are short of the questions those dashboards do not answer: is the unit working, can a visitor pay without an app, is the bay blocked, and can a disabled driver use it? Official statistics count public EV chargers. They do not count completed sessions. The government’s own National Chargepoint Registry — the nearest thing to a single planning database — was decommissioned on 28 November 2024.

Short answer
Plan from power band, location type, open-data status and operator reliability reports — not from a raw pin count. In England, LEVI funds on-street charging for residents without off-street parking, but installations still lag the allocated capital. Driver-facing checks are in our reliability guide; this page is for people who have to commission or oversee a local network.

What the official numbers do — and do not — measure

As of 1 July 2026 the Department for Transport counted 121,171 public EV chargers in the UK and 97,266 public charging devices (one cabinet can contain more than one EVSE). There were 174.9 public chargers per 100,000 people, and 28,887 rated 50kW or above. As of 1 April 2026 there was one public charger for every 17 battery electric vehicles. Source: Public electric vehicle charging infrastructure statistics: 1 July 2026 (official statistics in development; next quarterly release scheduled for November 2026).

The mix matters more than the headline:

  • 49% of chargers were standard (3kW to less than 8kW); 12% rapid; 12% ultra-rapid.
  • 52% destination, 33% on-street, 11% en-route, 4% other / semi-public.
  • In England and Wales, 16.7% of public chargers were in rural areas, against 17.5% of the population living there (ONS 2021 rural-urban classification, cited by DfT). Rural under-provision is therefore not mainly a headcount problem — it is more often speed, hours and repair times.

DfT also records a data-quality shock: in June 2026 one charge point operator removed about 1,270 EV chargers from its public open-data feed after they had been reported as public when they were private-access. A council that treated last quarter’s total as “installed infrastructure” would have been planning on hardware that was never available to residents.

The planning data gap after the National Chargepoint Registry

Until late 2024, councils and developers could pull a single National Chargepoint Registry dataset. GOV.UK’s Find and use data on public electric vehicle chargepoints page is now withdrawn. The registry was decommissioned on 28 November 2024. Archived data is available on request from OZEV (consumerofferconsult@ozev.gov.uk).

What replaced it is not another government map. Under the Public Charge Point Regulations 2023, each public operator must hold data in OCPI and publish reference data (location, connector, price, payment method, time restrictions) and availability data, free of charge, in a machine-readable form, without forcing users to accept terms on the data itself. Status changes must be reflected within 30 seconds. Government bodies and network operators can request the data. There is no official, free, national aggregator sitting on top of those feeds.

That is the “flying blind” problem in practical terms: planning teams now assemble a local picture from operator APIs, concession reports and DfT tables that still measure stock, not outcomes. Autodun’s map at ev.autodun.com is one independent view of public location data plus driver feedback — useful for spotting gaps, not a substitute for operator OCPI or DfT statistics.

LEVI, local strategies and who is responsible

The 2022 strategy Taking charge: the electric vehicle infrastructure strategy set out that local on-street charging should be planned so residents, businesses and visitors without off-street parking can charge without blocking pavements. DfT’s July 2026 statistical geography treats Local Transport Authorities in England as the bodies with responsibility for local transport planning and EV charging strategies (combined authorities where they exist, the Greater London Authority for London, upper-tier authorities elsewhere). OZEV’s help for local authorities page likewise says local authorities should write or take part in an EV infrastructure strategy, and that district and borough councils must work with the relevant highways authority even in two-tier areas. Some funding figures on that page pre-date LEVI delivery statistics — use the 2026 grant tables for current installation counts.

The Local Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (LEVI) Fund is the main English delivery programme for that on-street gap. It is capital plus capability funding. It applies to England. Applicants must show that the majority of chargepoints will benefit residents who do not have off-street parking.

Delivery is slower than the capital tables imply. OZEV’s grant scheme statistics (1 July 2026) record 643 LEVI-funded charger installations in England as of 1 April 2026, and a provisional 1,268 as of 1 July 2026 (the LEVI series runs about three months behind other charging statistics and the latest figure is subject to revision). Compare that with 121,171 public chargers nationwide: LEVI is still a small slice of the live network, even though regional capital allocations run to tens of millions of pounds (LEVI capital funding amounts, updated 30 July 2026).

Home and workplace grants sit with OZEV and are summarised on Electric vehicle chargepoint grants. They do not fill a high-street rapid gap, and they do not replace a local on-street plan.

Why a 99% rapid network can still fail a high street

The Public Charge Point Regulations 2023 require rapid networks (50kW+) to be reliable 99% of the time on average across the operator’s network in a calendar year. A blocked bay is exempt from that calculation, not scored as downtime. Slower on-street posts — the stock LEVI is meant to grow — are outside the 99% duty. Detail and the driver recovery steps are in EV charging reliability UK.

For a council concession, that means a contract that only restates “99% network reliability” can still leave a market town with one broken 7kW post for weeks. Procurement needs site-level uptime, repair-time targets, published helpline performance and open OCPI access — the duties operators already have toward OPSS — written into the local contract, not assumed from the national average.

OPSS enforces the regulations for OZEV. Maximum civil penalties are generally up to £10,000 per charge point for most duties, and up to £10,000 per rapid network for the reliability duty (OPSS enforcement guidance). That is a backstop, not a local performance regime.

Accessibility is still a specification, not a legal floor

PAS 1899:2022 (accessible public chargepoints) was sponsored by government and Motability and published by BSI. A GOV.UK announcement describes it as the definition of accessible design. It is not, by itself, a duty in the Public Charge Point Regulations. Local procurement is where the specification becomes real: bay width, cable weight, screen height, dropped kerbs, and whether accessibility data is in the open feed so maps can filter it.

Checklist for a local charging plan

  1. Split the stock by power band and location type using DfT EVCI tables (EVCI0102 for local authorities, EVCI0201 for power, EVCI0301 for destination / on-street / en-route) — not a single “number of chargers” KPI.
  2. Map residents without off-street parking against on-street provision. That is the LEVI test, not visitor rapid bays at the supermarket.
  3. Require live OCPI (reference + availability, 30-second status, p/kWh, payment methods) in every concession, including who may republish it.
  4. Write site-level repair times for slow AC as well as rapid DC. Do not rely on the 99% rapid network average.
  5. Score blocked bays separately. They are exempt from the legal reliability formula and are often the real failure mode on high streets.
  6. Put PAS 1899 into the specification where you control the land, and require accessibility fields in the data feed.
  7. Publish the operator helpline on council pages for each concession, and a route for residents to escalate to OPSS.
  8. Reconcile quarterly DfT figures with your concession inventory. If they diverge, find out whether units are private-access, decommissioned or missing from open data — DfT has already had to correct thousands of mis-tagged chargers in 2026.
  9. Do not treat a consumer map as a statutory dataset. Use it as a field check. For how drivers should read maps, see how to find UK charge points.

Frequently asked questions

Is there still a National Chargepoint Registry for UK councils to use?

No. GOV.UK withdrew the National Chargepoint Registry guidance on 2 December 2024. The registry was decommissioned on 28 November 2024. Public operators must instead share open data (location, availability, connectors, payment methods) under the Public Charge Point Regulations 2023.

Do official charger counts show whether units actually work?

No. Department for Transport statistics count public EV chargers (EVSE) and devices. They do not measure completed charging sessions. The 99% reliability duty applies only as a network average for rapid chargers of 50kW and above.

What is the LEVI Fund for?

The Local Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (LEVI) Fund supports local authorities in England to plan and deliver charging for residents without off-street parking. It has capital funding for chargepoints and capability funding for staff. It does not apply to Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland under the same scheme.

Who is responsible for local EV charging strategies in England?

DfT’s July 2026 statistics present England using Local Transport Authorities, which it describes as having responsibility for local transport planning and EV charging strategies — combined authorities where they exist, the Greater London Authority for London, and upper-tier authorities elsewhere.
