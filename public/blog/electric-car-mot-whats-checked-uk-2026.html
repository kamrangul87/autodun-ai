Electric Car MOT (UK 2026): What’s Checked — and What Isn’t

By the Autodun Team · Updated 4 November 2026 · Verified against GOV.UK and the DVSA MOT inspection manual

Short answer: yes, electric cars need an MOT in the UK once they turn 3 years old, then every year after that — exactly like petrol and diesel cars (GOV.UK). What’s different is mainly what is not tested: there’s no exhaust emissions or exhaust system check on a pure EV. Testers do carry out normal safety inspections (brakes, tyres, lights, steering, suspension) and visual checks of high‑voltage (HV) cables and the charging port using DVSA guidance for alternative‑fuel vehicles.

Do electric cars need an MOT?

Yes. Your EV’s first MOT is due on the third anniversary of first registration, then every 12 months (GOV.UK – Getting an MOT). The only vehicles that skip emissions testing are hybrids and hydrogen fuel cell vehicles (explicitly listed), and pure EVs have no tailpipe to test — but the MOT itself still applies.

What’s checked on an EV MOT

The EV MOT covers the same roadworthiness items as any car, plus EV‑specific visual checks. Expect the tester to examine:

  • Brakes — service/parking brake performance and balance; ABS/EPB where fitted (DVSA manual — Brakes).
  • Tyres & wheels — tread depth, condition, size and type.
  • Lights, electrics & visibility — headlamps, indicators, stop lamps, horn, wipers/washers.
  • Steering & suspension — wear, leaks and play.
  • Structure & body — corrosion, sharp edges, number plates, VIN.
  • High‑voltage cabling & charging inlet (visual) — routing, chafing/damage, connector security (DVSA alternative‑fuel guidance).

Important: DVSA guidance notes that EV MOTs do not measure battery health or range. Battery degradation is not part of the MOT scope.

What’s not checked on a pure EV

  • Exhaust emissions test — not applicable (no tailpipe). Hybrids and hydrogen FCEVs are also excluded from certain emissions checks (DVSA manual — Nuisance/Emissions).
  • Exhaust system/catalyst/DPF — not present on BEVs.
  • Engine noise test — not applicable to BEVs.

EV vs hybrid: key differences

Hybrids and plug‑in hybrids (PHEVs) still undergo the standard emissions and exhaust checks for their combustion engine, in addition to the EV‑specific visual checks for HV cabling and, for PHEVs, the charging port (DVSA alternative‑fuel guidance).

How brake testing works on EVs

Brake performance is normally tested on a roller brake tester (RBT). Where the vehicle’s drive configuration, transmission or braking system makes this unsuitable, testers may use a decelerometer for service/secondary brakes. Electronic parking brake (EPB) performance is not assessed by decelerometer — use the approved RBT in manual mode where specified (DVSA manual — Brakes and manual updates).

Costs and retests (official rules)

  • Cost: Same cap as any Class 4 car — £54.85 maximum in 2026 (many garages charge less). Source: GOV.UK — MOT test fees.
  • Retests: Leave the car at the same centre and retest within 10 working days → no fee for the partial retest. If you take it away and return within 10 working days, a partial retest fee (up to half the max) may be charged. Sources: GOV.UK — Retest after a repair, MOT testing guide — Accounts & fees.

EV MOT FAQ

Do electric cars need an MOT?

Yes. First MOT at 3 years, then annually (GOV.UK).

Is the EV battery health tested?

No. Testers do not measure battery degradation or state of health during the MOT.

Is there an emissions test?

No for BEVs; hybrids and hydrogen vehicles are also excluded from specific emissions checks in the manual (DVSA manual — Emissions).

How much is an EV MOT?

Up to £54.85 (Class 4 car). Many centres charge less (GOV.UK).

What about retests?

Leave it at the centre and retest within 10 working days → free partial retest. If taken away and returned within 10 working days → partial retest fee may apply (up to half the max). See GOV.UK — Retests.
