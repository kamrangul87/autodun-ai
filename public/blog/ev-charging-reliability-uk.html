EV charging reliability in the UK: what the 99% rule covers — and what it doesn’t

By Autodun Research Team · Updated 9 November 2026 · Rules checked against the Public Charge Point Regulations 2023

Finding a charger on a map is not the same as completing a charge. In UK law, “99% reliable” applies to an operator’s rapid network as a whole (50kW and above), averaged over a calendar year — not to the post in front of you, and not to slower on-street units. That is set out in the Public Charge Point Regulations 2023 and OZEV’s operator guidance.

Short answer
Treat live status as a clue. Prefer sites with several bays, confirm contactless or another payment method you already have, and keep enough range to reach a backup. If the unit fails, use the free 24/7 helpline on the cabinet. Motorway-specific planning is in our motorway charging guide; how maps work is in how to find UK charge points.

Why “covered on the map” is not reliability

The Department for Transport’s public EV charging statistics (1 July 2026) counted 121,171 public EV chargers in the UK (174.9 per 100,000 people), of which 28,887 were rated rapid or above (50kW+). 49% of all public chargers were in the slowest band (3kW to less than 8kW). Only 11% were classed as en-route.

Those figures are official statistics in development, collated by Zapmap from operator open data under the 2023 regulations. They count devices, not completed sessions. DfT also notes that in June 2026 one operator removed about 1,270 chargers that had been reported as public when they were private-access — a reminder that even the national dashboard can move when data quality is corrected.

London illustrates the gap between “lots of pins” and “a rapid charge when you need one”: 345.1 public chargers per 100,000 people, but only 27.9 rapid-or-above per 100,000 — the second-lowest rapid density in the UK table, because much of the stock is standard on-street charging (same DfT release, Annex A).

What the 99% duty actually measures

From 24 November 2024, charge point operators must keep their rapid public network (50kW and above) reliable for 99% of the time on average across that network in each calendar year. A weak month can be offset later in the year. Operators must publish compliance on their website and send an annual report to the Secretary of State and the enforcement authority. Source: PCPR 2023 guidance — Reliability and The Public Charge Point Regulations 2023.

Status is measured per EVSE (one vehicle at a time) using OCPI:

Counts as OCPI status What it means for you
Working (uptime) Available, Charging, Reserved The unit can start or continue a session, including contactless where that is required.
Downtime Inoperative, Out of order Software or hardware fault, including a broken contactless terminal (unless the site defaults to free charging).
Exempt from the calculation Blocked, Planned, Removed, Unknown A physical barrier — including another vehicle — is not counted as downtime. Unknown is for evidenced events outside the operator’s control, such as a grid or comms failure.

That “blocked is exempt” rule is why a unit can be on a map, inside a 99% network, and still be useless when you arrive. Guidance also says operators should not allow lower-used points, including in rural areas, to sit regularly out of order — but the legal 99% figure remains a network average, not a promise about the bay you chose.

Status changes must appear in the open data within 30 seconds. Maps can still lag if an app refreshes slowly, so a second nearby site remains the practical backup.

Payment, price and the helpline

The same regulations set consumer rules that affect whether a session actually starts:

  • Contactless: new public charge points of 8kW and above deployed after 24 November 2024, and all public charge points of 50kW and above, must offer contactless without a pre-existing contract. Bank cards must be accepted, not only Apple Pay or Google Pay. Free charge points are out of scope.
  • Price: the maximum session price must be shown clearly in pence (or pounds) per kWh, including fixed connection or transaction fees in that p/kWh figure. The price must not rise above the advertised maximum after you start.
  • Helpline: a free, staffed telephone line, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, advertised at the site. Voicemail and numbers that charge local call rates do not meet the rule. Operators are not required to recover a stranded vehicle.
  • Roaming: operators must let you pay through at least one third-party roaming provider (from 24 November 2025). Apple Pay or Google Pay alone does not count as roaming.

Micro-business operators are generally out of scope except for the pricing rule. Workplace-only and residential-only units are not “public” for these regulations.

How to plan a charge that usually works

  1. Decide the job. A 20-minute top-up needs rapid DC (50kW+). A supermarket stay can use slower AC. Matching speed to dwell time avoids the wrong queue.
  2. Pick a cluster, not a pin. If the first bay is blocked or inoperative, you need another unit within a short drive. This is the same habit we recommend on motorways, applied to town centres and retail parks.
  3. Confirm payment before you rely on the site. Look for contactless on 8kW+ (new) and 50kW+ (all). Carry a second method (roaming app or RFID) for older slow posts.
  4. Read access rules. Hotel, workplace and gated car parks may appear on maps while remaining closed to the public. PCPR only covers charge points intended for public access.
  5. Arrive with margin. Keep enough range to reach the backup. The helpline will not recover you if you are stranded.
  6. Check the operator’s published reliability page for frequent rapid networks you use. The 99% report is a network figure; still useful for spotting operators that miss the duty.

How to report a broken charger

  1. Note the operator name, the unit ID on the cabinet, time, and what failed (no power, connector jammed, payment declined, bay blocked).
  2. Call the free 24/7 helpline printed on or at the charge point. That is the first-line duty under the regulations.
  3. If contactless is required at that unit and it is dead, that counts as downtime for the operator’s reliability calculation (unless the site is giving energy away for free).
  4. For suspected repeated breaches (no helpline, no contactless where required, price not in p/kWh, open data missing), contact the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), which enforces the regulations for OZEV: opss.enquiries@businessandtrade.gov.uk, helpdesk 0121 345 1201. Maximum civil penalties are set in the regulations (generally up to £10,000 per charge point for most duties, and up to £10,000 per rapid network for the 99% reliability duty). See OPSS enforcement actions.

Councils planning networks from charger counts, rather than session success, is a different problem — that is why UK councils struggle to plan from charger counts.

Reliability checklist

  • Filter to the speed you actually need (rapid DC vs slow AC).
  • Choose a site with more than one compatible connector.
  • Confirm opening hours and whether the site is truly public.
  • Have contactless plus one roaming or app backup.
  • Keep range to reach a second site.
  • If it fails: helpline first, then OPSS for a suspected regulatory breach.

Frequently asked questions

Does every UK public charger have to be 99% reliable?

No. The Public Charge Point Regulations 2023 require 99% reliability as an average across each operator’s network of rapid charge points (50kW and above) over the calendar year. Slower on-street and destination chargers are not covered by that 99% duty.

Why can a city look full of chargers but still be hard to use?

Official counts measure EV chargers (EVSE), not completed sessions. A pin can be slow AC, behind opening hours, needing an app, or blocked by another vehicle. Under the reliability rules, a physically blocked rapid unit is treated as exempt from the uptime calculation, not as downtime.

Do I need an app to pay at a public charger?

Not always. GOV.UK says new public charge points of 8kW and above, and existing charge points of 50kW and above, must offer contactless payment without a pre-existing contract. Free charge points are out of scope. Slower or older units may still need an app or RFID card.

How do I report a broken public charge point?

Call the free 24/7 staffed helpline displayed at the site. Operators must offer real-time assistance, not voicemail. If you suspect a breach of the Public Charge Point Regulations, contact the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), which enforces the rules for OZEV.
