MOT advisories explained: Dangerous, Major, Minor and what to fix

By Autodun Research Team · Updated 9 November 2026 · Categories verified against DVSA and GOV.UK

An MOT advisory is not a fail. Since 20 May 2018, testers in Great Britain record defects as Dangerous, Major or Minor, and they can still add advisories for items that are not yet bad enough to reject. Dangerous and Major items fail the test. Minor defects and advisories still pass. That split is set out by the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency (DVSA) in MOT rule changes: 20 May 2018 and the MOT inspection manual.

Short answer
Read the category on the certificate, not the garage’s sales pitch. Advisories: monitor and repair if necessary. Minor: repair as soon as possible (still a pass). Major: fail — repair immediately. Dangerous: fail — do not drive until it is repaired. Confirm the wording on gov.uk/check-mot-history.

What each MOT category means

DVSA’s wording below is from the 20 May 2018 changes and the inspection manual introduction. Testers pick a pre-defined defect from the MOT testing service. They do not invent the category.

Result What DVSA says it means MOT result
Dangerous A direct and immediate risk to road safety or a serious impact on the environment. Do not drive the vehicle until it has been repaired. Fail
Major It may affect the vehicle’s safety, put other road users at risk or have an impact on the environment. Repair it immediately. Fail
Minor No significant effect on the safety of the vehicle or impact on the environment. Repair as soon as possible. Pass
Advisory It could become more serious in the future. Monitor and repair it if necessary. Pass

Source: GOV.UK — MOT rule changes: 20 May 2018. The inspection manual adds that testers must record Minor defects where they apply, while an advisory is used when an item is not yet bad enough to reject (MOT inspection manual — Introduction).

Northern Ireland uses a different MOT process. This guide covers Great Britain (England, Scotland and Wales). If the vehicle was tested in Northern Ireland, start with GOV.UK MOT history and NI Direct for certificates.

Can you drive after the test?

GOV.UK’s MOT test result page is the rule to follow:

  • If the vehicle passes, you can drive. You might also get a list of minor or advisory problems to monitor or fix later.
  • If it fails because of Dangerous or Major problems, you might not be allowed to drive until you fix them.
  • You can take a failed vehicle away only if your current MOT is still valid and no dangerous problems were listed. Otherwise, get it repaired before you drive.
  • Driving a vehicle that failed because of a dangerous problem can mean a fine of up to £2,500, a driving ban and 3 penalty points.

Separately, you can be fined up to £1,000 for driving without a valid MOT, except to a pre-arranged MOT test or to or from somewhere to be repaired (Getting an MOT). An MOT pass does not prove the vehicle will stay roadworthy until the next test. GOV.UK’s Check your vehicle is safe to drive page says you are responsible for roadworthiness at all times, and that driving a vehicle in a dangerous condition can also mean a £2,500 fine, a ban and 3 points.

If the test failed and you need the retest rules (free partial retest if the vehicle stays at the same centre and is retested within 10 working days), see MOT retest costs UK 2026 and GOV.UK — Retest after a repair.

How to read advisories on GOV.UK

This is the official check, not a garage printout. Steps follow Check the MOT history of a vehicle. For the full walkthrough of mileage, expiry and red flags, use our MOT history check guide — this page is only about classifying defects.

  1. Open gov.uk/check-mot-history and enter the registration number.
  2. Open the latest test. Note whether it passed or failed, the mileage, and the due date.
  3. For tests in England, Scotland or Wales, GOV.UK says you can see what parts failed and if any parts had minor problems. Match each line to Dangerous, Major, Minor or Advisory.
  4. Scroll earlier tests. Look for the same component repeating, or an advisory that later becomes a fail.
  5. Do not treat Autodun, a dealer listing or a paper invoice as the source of truth. The DVSA record is.

Coverage on GOV.UK: cars, motorcycles and vans from tests since 2005; HGVs, trailers, buses and coaches since 2018; Northern Ireland tests since 2017. Advisories are not a rolling window — they stay on the tests where they were recorded.

A common example: car tyres. GOV.UK (Check your vehicle is safe to drive and the Highway Code, Annex 6) says cars, light vans and light trailers must have at least 1.6 mm tread across the central three-quarters of the breadth of the tread and around the entire circumference. The inspection manual records tyre tread depth not in accordance with the requirements as Dangerous (MOT inspection manual — tyres). A tyre that is wearing but still above the limit may be an advisory. Below the limit is not “something to watch”.

Owner checklist: what to do next

  1. Keep the document. Ask for a printed certificate or refusal notice, and check the same result on GOV.UK.
  2. Sort the list by category. Dangerous first, then Major, then Minor, then Advisory.
  3. Do not drive with a Dangerous defect. Arrange recovery or on-site repair.
  4. If it failed with only Major items and the previous MOT is still in date, you may drive it away — it must still be roadworthy. Book the repair and retest.
  5. Get the repair in writing with the defect wording copied from the certificate. That makes a later retest and a used-car sale easier to evidence.
  6. For advisories, decide based on the component: lights and wipers are usually cheap to sort; tyres, brakes, steering and corrosion are the ones that tend to escalate. We do not publish unverified typical repair prices — ask more than one garage.
  7. Retest rules depend on whether the vehicle left the premises and on the 10 working-day window. See retest costs.
  8. Fees: the maximum Class 4 car MOT fee is £54.85, with no VAT on the fee (GOV.UK — MOT costs). Garages may charge less. Repair work is separate and is not capped by that figure.
Garage language vs the certificate
“Needs doing today” is not a legal category. If the certificate says Advisory or Minor, there is no MOT-law duty to repair before you drive away. You still have a roadworthiness duty. If you are unsure, ask the tester to show you the defect on the MOT testing service wording, then compare it with GOV.UK.

Using advisories when you buy a used car

Pull the official history before you view the car. A single advisory for light tyre wear on an otherwise clean record is ordinary. The same advisory repeated for several years, or an advisory that later became a Major fail, is a maintenance pattern — not a surprise.

  • Same component on consecutive tests with no evidence of work
  • Advisory → Major or Dangerous on a later test
  • A recent Dangerous fail, even if the car later passed
  • Mileage that falls between tests (see the history guide)

Advisories are visible to anyone who checks GOV.UK, so they can affect a private sale. We have not seen an official statistic for “how much value they remove”, so we do not invent one. Use the listed items as a negotiating list: ask for receipts that match the wording, or reduce the offer by a written quote from an independent garage.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to fix MOT advisories before driving away?

No. An advisory does not fail the MOT. GOV.UK says you might get a list of minor or advisory problems to monitor or fix in the future. You still have to keep the vehicle roadworthy. Dangerous defects are different: do not drive until they are repaired.

What is the difference between an MOT advisory and a Minor defect?

Both still pass the test. DVSA’s MOT inspection manual says a Minor defect is a recorded defect with no significant effect on safety or the environment, and testers must record it. An advisory is used when an item is not yet bad enough to reject. Monitor it and repair it if necessary.

Can I drive if my MOT failed with a Major defect?

Only if your current MOT is still valid and the result lists no dangerous problems. GOV.UK (After the test) says otherwise you must get it repaired before you can drive. Driving a vehicle that failed because of a dangerous problem can mean a fine of up to £2,500, a driving ban and 3 penalty points.

How do I check what advisories a vehicle has?

Use the free GOV.UK MOT history service at gov.uk/check-mot-history with the registration number. For tests in England, Scotland or Wales you can see what parts failed and if any parts had minor problems. Car, motorcycle and van results go back to 2005.

Can an advisory become a failure at the next MOT?

Yes. DVSA describes an advisory as something that could become more serious in the future. Wear items such as tyres and brakes often move from advisory to a fail if they are left. Check the wording on the certificate and compare it with later tests on GOV.UK.
